\begin{abstract}
\noindent Hidden Markov models (HMMs) occupy a storied place in quantitative
finance: the Baum--Welch algorithm was co-invented by Leonard Baum, Jim
Simons' first partner at Renaissance Technologies. We ask whether the
machinery pays its way out of sample---and, when it does, \emph{which
ingredient} is doing the work: the hidden Markov dynamics, or merely the fat
tails of the implied mixture distribution. We evaluate seven models
(Gaussian and Student-$t$ HMMs, rule-based volatility regimes, RiskMetrics
EWMA, rolling variance, and GARCH(1,1) with Normal and Student-$t$
innovations) on the S\&P~500 and NASDAQ (1999--2026) and WTI crude
(1986--2026), in an expanding-window walk-forward covering 5{,}600--8{,}900
out-of-sample days per asset that include the 2008 crisis, COVID-2020, and
2022. Models are scored on variance point forecasts (QLIKE, with
Diebold--Mariano tests and Hansen--Lunde--Nason model confidence sets), full
predictive densities (log score, Berkowitz calibration), and tail risk (VaR
at three levels with Kupiec/Christoffersen backtests; expected-shortfall
calibration ratios). Three findings emerge. \emph{First}, for variance point
forecasts GARCH(1,1) dominates and the HMMs trail significantly---regime
discreteness costs accuracy that no emission distribution repairs.
\emph{Second}, for tail risk the decisive ingredient is the tail, not the
hidden state: at a 1\% VaR the Student-$t$ HMM and GARCH-$t$ are the two
best-calibrated models on every asset and the only two whose coverage is
statistically correct on WTI, while every Gaussian model---including the
Gaussian HMM's own mixture---over-breaches; expected-shortfall ratios show
the same split (fat-tailed models 1.00--1.11, Gaussian models 1.11--1.25).
\emph{Third}, crisis windows reverse the picture in an instructive way: in
the 2008 collapse the HMMs breached a 1\% VaR on 7--8\% of S\&P days---their
volatility ceiling, estimated on pre-crisis history, could not reach
2008 levels---while recursive-variance models adapted within days. Hidden
states retain genuine descriptive value (they discover the leverage effect
unsupervised) and deliver the best full-density calibration on the
commodity; but the apparent tail-risk superiority of regime mixtures in
Gaussian-only comparisons is shown to come from their tails, not their
Markov dynamics. Volatility-targeting overlays improve equity Sharpe ratios
under every forecaster yet no improvement survives a stationary-bootstrap
test. Completing the arc, we field the hybrid these results motivate:
Markov-switching GARCH \citep{haas2004} --- persistent hidden states, each
carrying its own GARCH recursion. It becomes the first model in the study to
join GARCH-$t$ in every model confidence set on every asset, matches the
$t$-pair's tail coverage (on WTI, GARCH-$t$, the $t$-HMM and MS-GARCH-$t$
are all Kupiec-accepted at all three VaR levels; MS-GARCH-$t$ alone of the
three fails the Christoffersen independence test, at 1\%), and repairs
roughly half of the HMM's 2008 breach failure --- but it nowhere
\emph{beats} GARCH-$t$. Regime structure becomes
free once tails are fat and variance adapts; it does not become profitable.
Sophistication is not edge; the useful sophistication is a fat-tailed
innovation, and it is available for one extra parameter.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The romance of regime-switching models in finance begins with a
biographical fact: before founding Renaissance Technologies, Jim Simons
partnered with Leonard Baum, co-inventor of the algorithm \citep{baum1970}
that made hidden Markov models estimable. The intellectual bet---that
markets, like speech, are driven by unobservable states inferable from
noisy observations---has attracted researchers ever since
\citep{hamilton1989, ryden1998, ang2002, guidolin2007, nystrup2018}.

This paper asks the question an allocator (or a sceptical PhD committee)
would ask---\emph{does the machinery pay its way out of sample?}---and
pushes it one step further than the usual horse race. A hidden-Markov
mixture differs from a one-line variance recursion in two separable ways:
its \emph{dynamics} (a discrete persistent chain rather than a smooth
filter) and its \emph{distribution} (a mixture, hence fat-tailed, rather
than a Gaussian). Comparing a Gaussian HMM only against Gaussian filters
confounds the two. We therefore field a $2\times2$-style design: Gaussian
and Student-$t$ HMMs against GARCH(1,1) with Normal and Student-$t$
innovations, alongside three simpler baselines, so that every claim about
``regimes'' can be checked against a matched-tail alternative.

We also insist that ``forecasting volatility'' is not one task. A model may
forecast the \emph{level} of tomorrow's variance poorly while describing
the \emph{shape} of tomorrow's distribution well; for a risk manager the
shape---especially the left tail---is the object that matters. Every model
is scored on three progressively richer objects: variance point forecasts
under QLIKE loss \citep{patton2011}, one-day-ahead densities under the log
score with Berkowitz calibration tests \citep{berkowitz2001}, and Value-at-
Risk at three levels with coverage and independence backtests
\citep{kupiec1995, christoffersen1998}, supplemented by expected-shortfall
calibration ratios. Point- and density-forecast comparisons are
disciplined by Diebold--Mariano tests \citep{diebold1995} and
Hansen--Lunde--Nason model confidence sets \citep{hansen2011}.

Our contribution is an honest accounting in the spirit of
\citet{hansen2005}, extended to a decomposition: when regime models look
good, is it the regimes or the tails? The answer, on twenty-seven years of
equity data and forty of commodity data, is: the tails.

\section{Models}
\subsection{Hidden Markov models}
Daily returns $r_t$ are modelled as draws from one of $K=3$ emission
distributions selected by a latent first-order Markov chain with transition
matrix $A$. The \textbf{Gaussian HMM} uses
$\mathcal{N}(\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)$ emissions, estimated by Baum--Welch
(EM over scaled forward--backward recursions) implemented from scratch;
states are decoded by Viterbi and relabelled by ascending risk.
The \textbf{Student-$t$ HMM} replaces each emission with a location-scale
$t$: $r_t \sim \mu_k + \sigma_k\, t(\nu_k)$, estimated by the classical
normal/inverse-gamma EM augmentation (per-observation tail weights
$u_{tk}=(\nu_k+1)/(\nu_k+z_{tk}^2)$), with $\nu_k$ updated by profile
likelihood. Fat tails \emph{inside} each regime let the model separate
``the regime got riskier'' from ``a single outlier landed''.

Both implementations are validated on synthetic data with known parameters
before touching market data: the Gaussian version recovers a known hidden
path with $>85\%$ Viterbi accuracy; the $t$ version correctly identifies
which synthetic state is fat-tailed ($\hat\nu$ ordering) and reproduces its
own quantiles with correct empirical coverage.

For out-of-sample use we maintain filtered probabilities
$p_t = P(s_t\mid r_{1:t})$ recursively; with predictive weights $q=p_tA$
the one-day density is the mixture $\sum_k q_k f_k$, its variance follows
from mixture moments, its VaR by bisection on the mixture CDF, and its
expected shortfall in closed form from component partial expectations.

\subsection{Comparators}
\textbf{Rule-based regimes}: calm/normal/turbulent by terciles of trailing
21-day realised volatility (edges from training data only); forecast = the
current regime's training-pool variance. The observable-state analogue of
the HMM. \textbf{EWMA}: $\hat v_{t+1}=0.94\hat v_t + 0.06 r_t^2$
\citep{riskmetrics1996}. \textbf{Rolling}: 21-day trailing variance.
\textbf{GARCH(1,1)-Normal} and \textbf{GARCH(1,1)-$t$}
\citep{bollerslev1986}: $\hat v_{t+1}=\omega+\alpha r_t^2+\beta\hat v_t$
with Normal or standardised-$t(\nu)$ innovations, estimated by maximum
likelihood at each refit; between refits the recursion runs with fixed
parameters. Density, VaR and ES for the $t$ variant use the scaled-$t$
closed forms. For Gaussian-density models the log score is an affine
transform of QLIKE, so their density ranking must equal their QLIKE
ranking; only the mixture and $t$ models can genuinely differ across
scoring rules.

\subsection{The hybrid: Markov-switching GARCH}\label{sec:hybridmodel}
Finally we field the model our own results motivate. \textbf{MS-GARCH}
\citep{haas2004} combines both ingredients: a persistent two-state hidden
Markov chain in which \emph{each} regime carries its own GARCH(1,1)
recursion, $h_{k,t} = \omega_k + \alpha_k r_{t-1}^2 + \beta_k h_{k,t-1}$,
with Normal or standardised Student-$t$ innovations. The
Haas--Mittnik--Paolella specification lets every regime's variance evolve
in parallel off the observed return, so the likelihood is computable
exactly by the Hamilton filter without integrating over $K^T$ variance
paths. We implement it from scratch ($K{=}2$, MLE on the filtered
likelihood, regimes ordered calm-first) and verify, before touching market
data, that the implementation recovers the regimes, persistences, and
transition matrix of a \emph{known} simulated MS-GARCH. Out of sample it
refits annually with warm starts---GARCH-type recursions adapt daily by
construction, so frequent re-estimation is unnecessary---and its
predictive density is a two-component mixture whose VaR and ES follow from
the same closed forms as the HMM mixtures.

\section{Data and protocol}
Data are daily closes: S\&P~500 and NASDAQ composite, January 1999--June
2026 (6{,}913 returns each; Yahoo Finance), and WTI crude spot, January
1986--June 2026 (10{,}189 returns; FRED \texttt{DCOILWTICO}); the exact
series used are cached in the repository. The first 1{,}260 days form the
initial window; all seven models are re-estimated every 63 trading days on
an expanding window; filters and recursions update daily; every forecast of
day $t{+}1$ uses only information through day $t$. This yields 5{,}653
out-of-sample days per equity index (2004--2026: covering 2008, COVID-2020,
and 2022) and 8{,}929 for WTI (1991--2026). Statistical machinery: QLIKE
$L_t=\log\hat v_t + r_t^2/\hat v_t$; Diebold--Mariano with Bartlett/HAC
variance (5 lags); model confidence sets at the 10\% level by stationary
block bootstrap \citep{hansen2011, politis1994}; Kupiec
proportion-of-failures and Christoffersen independence tests at VaR levels
$\{1\%, 2.5\%, 5\%\}$; ES calibration as the ratio of mean realised loss
beyond VaR to mean predicted ES on breach days (1 = calibrated); Berkowitz
LR test on probability integral transforms.

\section{Results}
\subsection{What the hidden states discover}
Fitted to the full 1999--2026 S\&P sample, the Gaussian HMM's states remain
economically interpretable rather than mere volatility buckets
(Table~\ref{tab:states}): the calm state combines a $+11.0$\,bp daily drift
with 8.8\% annualised volatility; the turbulent state $-15.2$\,bp with
45.6\%. The leverage effect is discovered unsupervised, consistent with
\citet{ryden1998}. The chain still never jumps directly between calm and
turbulent. The $t$-HMM adds a sharper observation: conditional on the
regime, returns remain fat-tailed---$\hat\nu\approx7$ in the calm state and
$\hat\nu\approx6$ in the turbulent state---so regime switching alone does
not exhaust the non-Gaussianity of daily returns. This foreshadows the
tail-risk results below.

\subsection{Variance point forecasts: recursions win, tails don't help}
Table~\ref{tab:qlike} reports QLIKE. GARCH is best on all three assets, and
the innovation distribution is nearly irrelevant to the point forecast
(GARCH-N vs GARCH-$t$: indistinguishable on WTI, GARCH-N marginally ahead
on the equities)---as expected, since QLIKE scores only the variance path.
Both HMMs trail GARCH-$t$ significantly (DM $t=2.3$--$4.3$), and the
$t$-emissions do not repair the gap: discreteness, not the emission
distribution, is the binding constraint. The piecewise-constant forecasters
(rolling, rule-based) are decisively worst. Model confidence sets tell a
sample-dependent story: on NASDAQ the MCS retains \emph{only} GARCH-N; on
the S\&P and WTI---where the out-of-sample span includes 2008 and COVID
volatility of unprecedented scale---the data cannot separate the top six,
and only the rule-based forecaster is eliminated.

\subsection{Densities: GARCH-$t$ first, $t$-HMM second}
On the log score (Table~\ref{tab:logscore}) the fat-tailed pair pulls away
from everything Gaussian. GARCH-$t$ is the sole member of the model
confidence set on the S\&P and WTI and shares a two-member set with the
$t$-HMM on NASDAQ. The $t$-HMM is second everywhere---ahead of every
Gaussian model including GARCH-N---and the Gaussian HMM beats the Gaussian
filters on WTI only. Berkowitz calibration tests sharpen the split: on WTI
the two HMMs deliver the best-calibrated densities in the study
($p=0.87$ Gaussian, $p=0.95$ $t$; GARCH-N $p=0.48$, GARCH-$t$ $p=0.16$;
all simple filters rejected at $p<10^{-10}$), whereas on the equity
indices---whose out-of-sample span includes two once-a-generation
crises---\emph{every} model's density is rejected. Nothing in this
comparison set fully describes equity returns through 2008 and 2020; the
fat-tailed models merely fail least.

\subsection{Tail risk: fat tails, not hidden states}
Table~\ref{tab:var} is the paper's central evidence. At the 1\% level the
two Student-$t$ models are the best calibrated on every asset, and on WTI
they are the only two whose coverage passes the Kupiec test (GARCH-$t$
hit rate 1.00\%, $p=0.98$; HMM-$t$ 1.09\%, $p=0.42$). Every
Gaussian-density model over-breaches everywhere---\emph{including the
Gaussian HMM}, whose mixture provides some tail mass but not enough. On
the equity indices, whose evaluation span contains 2008 and 2020, no model
attains correct 1\% coverage, but the ordering is unchanged: $t$-tails
first (hit rates 1.4\%--1.7\%), Gaussian mixtures next, Gaussian
recursions last (up to 2.7\%---nearly triple the promised breach rate).
At the 5\% level, where tail shape matters less, most models pass and the
differences fade, confirming that the 1\% results are about distribution
tails rather than variance levels. Expected-shortfall calibration tells
the same story with severity instead of frequency: on breach days the
fat-tailed pair's ES ratios sit at 1.00--1.11 across assets while the
Gaussian models' realised losses run 11--25\% beyond their predicted ES.

The decomposition is now clean. In a Gaussian-only comparison (as in an
earlier version of this study), the Gaussian HMM appears to be the best
tail model available and one might credit the hidden states. Field a
matched-tail benchmark and the credit moves: \textbf{one extra degree of
freedom---$\nu$---in a one-line GARCH recursion buys tail calibration
equal to (on WTI, slightly better than) the full Baum--Welch apparatus.}
The hidden states are not what fixes the tail; the tail is.

\subsection{Crisis windows: where the state ceiling binds}
Sub-sampling the 1\%-VaR breaches to three stress windows exposes the
HMMs' characteristic failure mode (Table~\ref{tab:crisis}). In the second
half of 2008 on the S\&P, the Gaussian and $t$ HMMs breached on 7.9\% and
6.7\% of days respectively---seven times their nominal rate---while
GARCH-$t$ breached on 1.2\% and even the humble EWMA on 2.0\%. The
mechanism is structural: an HMM's worst-case one-day distribution is
bounded by its most turbulent state, whose $\sigma_k$ was estimated on
\emph{pre-2008} history; when realised volatility blew through every level
in the training sample, the discrete state ceiling bound, whereas
recursive-variance models scale without limit and adapted within days. By
COVID-2020 the HMMs had a 2008 in their training window and their breach
rates (4.0\%) fell in line with the pack; in 2022 every model was
calibrated. The same pattern appears in the WTI COVID window (which
contains the negative-price episode): recursions 3.2\%, HMMs 5.6--6.4\%.
Regime models learn new regimes only after living through them.

\subsection{State-count sensitivity}\label{sec:k}
Re-running the S\&P walk-forward with $K\in\{2,3,4,5,6,8\}$ Gaussian
states (refit every 126 days) improves both QLIKE and log score
monotonically in $K$, with the gains plateauing beyond $K\approx5$:
QLIKE moves from $-8.249$ ($K{=}2$) through $-8.344$ ($K{=}4$) to
$-8.383$ ($K{=}8$), closing roughly nine-tenths of the distance to the
GARCH benchmark ($-8.394$) but never crossing it. This is consistent with
the discretisation interpretation: a finer state grid approximates a
continuous volatility process ever better, converging toward---without
reaching---the smooth-recursion frontier. The three-state model's
point-forecast deficit is a resolution cost, not evidence against latent
structure per se; but no resolution within reach makes the latent chain a
\emph{better} point forecaster than the recursion it approximates.

\subsection{Volatility targeting, honestly tested}
Finally, a 10\%-vol-target overlay ($w_t=\min(1,
\sigma_{\text{tgt}}/\hat\sigma_t)$, 2\,bps per side on turnover;
\citealp{moreira2017}) improves the Sharpe ratio over buy-and-hold on both
equity indices under \emph{every} forecaster (S\&P: 0.54 $\to$ 0.58--0.67;
NASDAQ: 0.63 $\to$ 0.66--0.76) and cuts maximum drawdowns roughly in half
(S\&P $-57\%\to-25\%$; NASDAQ $-56\%\to-20\%$); on WTI (buy-and-hold
drawdown $-94\%$) the overlays reduce drawdown by 50+ points but lower the
Sharpe. A stationary-bootstrap test of each Sharpe difference rejects
nothing (minimum $p=0.09$, rule-based on NASDAQ). The drawdown reduction
is mechanical and large; the Sharpe improvement remains statistically
unproven at this sample size---and offers no wedge for sophisticated
forecasters over simple ones.

\subsection{The hybrid verdict: MS-GARCH closes both gaps, advances neither}
\label{sec:hybrid}
Sections 4.2--4.5 exposed two failure modes: the HMM's discreteness costs
point-forecast accuracy and its state ceilings break in unprecedented
crises, while everything thin-tailed fails extreme-quantile calibration.
MS-GARCH is the model theory nominates to fix both at once. We run the
Student-$t$ and Normal variants through the identical gauntlet against the
two incumbent champions (Table~\ref{tab:hybrid}). Three results.

\emph{First, the point-forecast gap closes completely.} MS-GARCH-$t$ is
the first model in this study that is statistically indistinguishable from
GARCH-$t$ on QLIKE on every asset (DM $t = 0.06$, $-1.0$, $1.5$; on NASDAQ
its point estimate is in fact the better one), and the first to join
GARCH-$t$ in \emph{every} 10\% model confidence set---QLIKE and log
score, all three assets---sets from which the $t$-HMM is excluded on five
of six occasions. Once within-regime variance adapts, regime structure no
longer costs accuracy. The three-state HMM's deficit was discreteness, not
the hidden chain itself.

\emph{Second, tail coverage matches the champion; 1\% independence does
not.} At the 1\% level MS-GARCH-$t$ passes Kupiec on WTI ($p=0.65$)
alongside GARCH-$t$ and the $t$-HMM. All three are accepted at
\emph{all three} VaR levels (Kupiec $p$ at 1\%/2.5\%/5\%: MS-GARCH-$t$
$0.65/0.83/0.71$, GARCH-$t$ $0.98/0.60/0.32$, $t$-HMM $0.42/0.70/0.28$),
so the hybrid is not unique here: the $t$ tail does the work, and the
Markov switching adds description, not prediction. On the Christoffersen
independence test it is the weakest of the three: at 1\% it fails
($p=0.0099$) where GARCH-$t$ ($p=0.074$) and the $t$-HMM ($p=0.116$) pass.
Its ES ratios (1.04--1.10) sit in the calibrated band of the $t$ pair. A curiosity with theoretical content: on WTI even
MS-GARCH-\emph{Normal} attains correct 1\% coverage ($p=0.72$)---mixing
Normals across regimes manufactures tail mass---but the substitute is
partial, failing at 2.5\% and 5\% and on both equities. Regime-switching
is a second route to fat tails; Student-$t$ innovations remain the robust
one.

\emph{Third, the crisis fragility is repaired---mostly.} In the 2008
window where the $t$-HMM breached a 1\% VaR on 6.7\% of S\&P days,
MS-GARCH-$t$ breached on 3.6\%---roughly half the failure---and through
COVID it matches GARCH-$t$ exactly on both the S\&P (3.2\%) and WTI
(3.2\% vs the HMM's 5.6\%). The residual 2008 gap to GARCH-$t$ (1.2\%)
shows that even adaptive regime variances react more slowly than a single
unbounded recursion when volatility explodes through every level in the
training history: the mixture must first reallocate probability mass to
the turbulent regime before that regime's recursion dominates the
predictive density.

The verdict is symmetric and, we think, the paper's most useful sentence:
\textbf{MS-GARCH joins the frontier without advancing it.} Nowhere does it
beat GARCH-$t$ at conventional significance, on any of the eight criteria.
Regime structure becomes \emph{free} once tails are fat and variance
adapts---a genuine improvement over the HMM, whose structure carried a
measurable cost---but on univariate volatility and tail-risk criteria it
does not become \emph{profitable}. Whatever premium hidden regimes may
command must lie where mixtures genuinely differ from single densities:
multi-horizon simulation, path-dependent risk, and regime-conditional
decision rules.

\section{Discussion}
The design separates two ingredients that regime-switching folklore
bundles together, and they land on opposite sides of the ledger.
\emph{The tail is first-order.} Moving from Normal to Student-$t$
innovations---one parameter---repairs VaR calibration for GARCH and
sharpens it for the HMM; no Gaussian model of any structural
sophistication achieves what either $t$ model achieves. \emph{The hidden
state is second-order for prediction.} It costs accuracy at the point
forecast (discreteness), adds little to tail calibration beyond what its
mixture tails already provide, and imposes a crisis-specific fragility:
state ceilings estimated on tranquil history cannot span an unprecedented
shock, precisely when a risk model earns its keep. What the hidden state
buys instead is \emph{understanding}: unsupervised discovery of the
leverage effect, persistent interpretable regimes, the no-direct-jump
topology of market stress, and---on the commodity---the best-calibrated
full density in the study. For a risk desk, the practical ranking is
GARCH-$t$ first for production, $t$-HMM close behind if regime
interpretation is wanted; for a researcher, the lesson is that any claimed
benefit of regime dynamics should be benchmarked against a matched-tail
alternative before the dynamics get the credit. The hybrid experiment
(Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}) sharpens this into a constructive statement:
give the regimes adaptive within-state variance and the structural cost
vanishes---MS-GARCH-$t$ ties the champion everywhere---yet no premium
appears. On univariate volatility and tail-risk criteria, regime dynamics
are at best free. The Renaissance lesson survives in amended form: Baum's
mathematics genuinely sees structure that filters do not---but seeing
structure and forecasting from it are different trades.

\section{Limitations}
(i) The crisis-window analysis is descriptive (three windows, small breach
counts). (ii) The MS-GARCH implementation uses $K{=}2$ and annual
warm-started refits; richer specifications (GJR asymmetry, $K{=}3$,
time-varying transition probabilities) could yet uncover a premium our
parsimonious variant misses. (iii) Quarterly refits for the other models
are a compute compromise. (iv) One-day horizon and long-only overlays;
multi-day distributions and derivatives applications may weight the
mixtures' full-path simulation abilities more heavily---and are the
natural place to look for the regime premium that one-day criteria do not
reveal. (v) Yahoo/FRED daily closes; results at intraday frequencies may
differ.

\section{Conclusion}
Evaluated honestly, out of sample, across assets and across three scoring
rules, hidden Markov models on daily returns are: significantly worse
point forecasters than GARCH; competitive density forecasters when given
$t$ emissions (and the best-calibrated densities on the commodity);
excellent tail-risk models---but no better than a GARCH-$t$ whose single
extra parameter supplies the same fat tail without the state ceiling that
failed in 2008. Fielding the theoretically-motivated hybrid completes the
account: Markov-switching GARCH inherits the recursion's accuracy and the
mixture's tails, repairs most of the crisis fragility, joins the model
confidence sets everywhere---and advances the frontier nowhere. The regime
machinery's durable contribution is descriptive. The durable practical
lesson is cheaper than the machinery: model the tail, and let the variance
be recursive.
