\begin{abstract}
\noindent Hidden Markov models (HMMs) occupy a storied place in quantitative
finance: the Baum--Welch algorithm was co-invented by Leonard Baum, Jim
Simons' first partner at Renaissance Technologies. We ask a deliberately
sceptical question: on daily S\&P~500 data, does a Gaussian HMM add
\emph{out-of-sample} value over embarrassingly simple alternatives? We find a
split verdict. As a \emph{descriptive} device the HMM is impressive: fitted
unsupervised on raw returns, it recovers three economically meaningful
states---a calm state that drifts up ($+9$\,bp/day, 9\% annualised
volatility) and a turbulent state that drifts down ($-12$\,bp/day, 42\%
volatility)---thereby discovering the leverage effect without being shown a
volatility feature. As a \emph{forecasting} device, however, it disappoints:
in an expanding-window walk-forward over 3{,}770 out-of-sample days, the
one-parameter RiskMetrics EWMA significantly outperforms the HMM's
predictive mixture at next-day volatility forecasting (QLIKE loss,
Diebold--Mariano $t=2.0$), and also beats rule-based regime forecasts and
rolling variance. A 10\%-volatility-targeting overlay net of costs improves
the Sharpe ratio over buy-and-hold for \emph{every} forecaster (0.38 to as
high as 0.51) and roughly halves maximum drawdown---but the simplest
forecasters deliver the largest gains. The value lies in the idea of
volatility targeting, not in sophisticated volatility estimation.
Sophistication, by itself, is not edge.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The romance of regime-switching models in finance begins with a
biographical fact: before founding Renaissance Technologies, Jim Simons
partnered with Leonard Baum, co-inventor of the algorithm
\citep{baum1970} that made hidden Markov models estimable. The intellectual
bet---that markets, like speech, are driven by unobservable states that can
be inferred from noisy observations---has attracted researchers ever since
\citep{hamilton1989, ryden1998, ang2002, guidolin2007, nystrup2018}.

This paper asks the question an allocator (or a sceptical PhD committee)
would ask: \emph{does the machinery pay its way out of sample?} We compare a
from-scratch Gaussian HMM against three deliberately humble alternatives on
twenty years of daily S\&P~500 data, under a protocol designed to preclude
hindsight: expanding-window walk-forward estimation, quarterly refits, an
online filter so every forecast at time $t$ uses only information through
$t$, robust loss functions, and formal forecast-comparison tests.

Our contribution is not a new model but an honest accounting, in the spirit
of \citet{hansen2005}, who famously asked whether anything beats a
GARCH(1,1). Our answer parallels theirs: for next-day volatility, nothing in
our regime-switching toolkit beats a 1994-vintage exponentially weighted
moving average \citep{riskmetrics1996}.

\section{Models}
\subsection{Gaussian hidden Markov model}
Daily returns $r_t$ are modelled as draws from one of $K=3$ Gaussian
emission distributions $\mathcal{N}(\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)$, selected by a latent
first-order Markov chain $s_t$ with transition matrix $A$. Parameters
$(\pi, A, \mu, \sigma)$ are estimated by Baum--Welch
(expectation--maximisation over the scaled forward--backward recursions),
implemented from scratch in \texttt{src/hmm.py}; the most likely state path
is decoded with the Viterbi algorithm. Initialisation is deterministic
(quantile splits of $|r_t|$), and states are relabelled by ascending
$\sigma_k$ so state~0 is always the calmest. Correctness is verified on
synthetic data with \emph{known} hidden states before any market data is
touched: the implementation recovers the true path with $>85\%$ Viterbi
accuracy and the true $\sigma_k$ within 15\%.

For out-of-sample prediction we maintain the filtered distribution
$p_t = P(s_t \mid r_{1:t})$ with a recursive filter. The predictive variance
of tomorrow's return under the mixture $q = p_t A$ is
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\text{Var}}_t(r_{t+1}) \;=\; \sum_k q_k(\sigma_k^2 + \mu_k^2)
\;-\; \Big(\sum_k q_k \mu_k\Big)^2 .
\end{equation}

\subsection{Comparators}
\textbf{Rule-based regimes.} Days are classified calm/normal/turbulent by
terciles of trailing 21-day realised volatility (edges estimated on training
data only); the forecast is the training-sample variance of the current
regime's return pool. This is the observable-state analogue of the HMM.
\textbf{EWMA.} $\hat v_{t+1} = 0.94\,\hat v_t + 0.06\, r_t^2$, the
RiskMetrics standard \citep{riskmetrics1996}. \textbf{Rolling.} 21-day
trailing variance.

\section{Data and protocol}
Data are daily S\&P~500 adjusted closes, January 1999--December 2018
($T=5{,}030$ returns), as bundled in the open-source \texttt{arch} package;
no vendor access is required. The first 1{,}260 days (five years) form the
initial training window. The HMM and the rule-based regime parameters are
re-estimated every 63 trading days on an expanding window; between refits
the HMM filter is updated daily. Every forecast of day $t{+}1$ volatility is
made strictly with information through day $t$. This yields 3{,}770
out-of-sample forecasts per method.

Forecasts are scored with the QLIKE loss
$L_t = \log \hat v_t + r_t^2/\hat v_t$, which is robust to noisy volatility
proxies \citep{patton2011}. Differences are tested with Diebold--Mariano
statistics using a Bartlett/HAC variance with five lags
\citep{diebold1995}.

\section{Results}
\subsection{What the HMM discovers}
Table~\ref{tab:states} reports the full-sample fit. The states are not mere
volatility buckets: mean returns fall monotonically as volatility rises. The
calm state combines a $+9.2$\,bp daily drift with 8.7\% annualised
volatility; the turbulent state combines a $-11.5$\,bp drift with 42.2\%
volatility. The model was never shown a volatility feature or a sign
asymmetry---it inferred the leverage effect from raw returns, consistent
with \citet{ryden1998}. All states are highly persistent (expected durations
of 32--49 trading days), and the chain never transits directly between calm
and turbulent: panic arrives, and recedes, through the intermediate state.
Decoded states agree with the rule-based labels on 60\% of days, so the
hidden states substantially---but not entirely---rediscover observable
volatility regimes.

\subsection{The forecasting shoot-out}
Table~\ref{tab:qlike} contains the paper's central result. The RiskMetrics
EWMA achieves the best (lowest) QLIKE, and the Diebold--Mariano tests reject
equal accuracy against every regime-based alternative: against the HMM
predictive mixture at $t=2.0$, against rule-based regime volatility at
$t=2.7$, and against rolling variance at $t=4.1$. A one-parameter filter
from 1996 beats the Baum--Welch machinery at its supposed home game.

The mechanism is not mysterious. Regime forecasts are, by construction,
piecewise-constant: within a regime the forecast barely moves, and at regime
boundaries it jumps between a small number of levels. Volatility itself
evolves continuously. The EWMA's geometric weighting tracks that continuous
evolution day by day, while the HMM must express all variation through
shifts in a three-point mixture.

\subsection{Does forecasting quality cash out? The vol-target overlay}
Following the logic of volatility-managed portfolios \citep{moreira2017}, we
run a 10\%-annualised-volatility-target overlay: the position in the index is
$w_t = \min\!\big(1, \sigma_{\text{tgt}}/\widehat{\sigma}_t\big)$, with
2\,bps per side charged on turnover. Table~\ref{tab:overlay} shows that
\emph{every} forecaster's overlay improves on buy-and-hold's Sharpe ratio
(0.38) and roughly halves its $-57\%$ maximum drawdown---and that the
ranking follows forecast accuracy: the simplest forecasters (rolling, EWMA,
Sharpe 0.51--0.52) beat the HMM overlay (0.42). Each overlay realises close
to its 10\% volatility target, so the comparison is at matched risk.

\section{Discussion}
The verdict is a clean split along the description/prediction boundary.
As a descriptive and generative device the HMM earns its reputation: it
discovers economically interpretable states unsupervised, and (as shown in
the companion simulation study in the same repository) regime-conditional
resampling reproduces the fat tails and volatility clustering that
Gaussian diffusion models miss entirely. As a short-horizon forecasting
device it does not: the filter-plus-mixture prediction is dominated by
exponential smoothing, echoing \citet{hansen2005} in a regime-switching
key. For the practitioner the actionable finding is
Table~\ref{tab:overlay}: volatility targeting is robustly valuable, and it
does not require---indeed is mildly hurt by---sophisticated state
inference. For the researcher, the finding is a caution against equating
model elegance with predictive content.

\section{Limitations}
(i)~One asset and one frequency; the HMM's relative standing may differ for
multi-asset problems, longer horizons, or applications that exploit the
full predictive \emph{distribution} rather than its variance.
(ii)~Gaussian emissions with $K=3$; richer emissions (Student-$t$) or
model-selected $K$ could narrow the gap. (iii)~Quarterly refits are a
compute compromise; daily refits are conceptually identical but were not
run. (iv)~We did not include a GARCH(1,1) comparator; given
\citet{hansen2005}, we expect it to perform near the EWMA, which is its
integrated special case. (v)~The overlay's improvement over buy-and-hold is
economic rather than formally tested; our significance claims concern
forecast accuracy only.

\section{Conclusion}
Fitted honestly and evaluated out of sample, a Gaussian hidden Markov model
on S\&P~500 returns is a very good storyteller and a mediocre forecaster.
The states it discovers are real and economically meaningful; the forecasts
it produces are significantly worse than a one-parameter EWMA; and the
robust practical value in this exercise---volatility targeting---is
available to anyone with a rolling window. The Renaissance lesson, at least
at this scale and with this data, is not that hidden-state models print
money; it is that disciplined evaluation separates what a model
\emph{reveals} from what it can \emph{predict}.
